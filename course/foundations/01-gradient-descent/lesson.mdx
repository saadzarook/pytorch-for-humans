## 1. 🤔 Why should I care?

You order a pizza. The app says **"arriving in 19 minutes"**. How does it know?

Somewhere, a computer looked at thousands of past deliveries and learned a rule. Let's shrink that down to something we can hold in our heads: **12 deliveries**, each with a distance and how long it took.

The simplest possible rule is a straight line:

```text
delivery time = w × distance + b
```

- **`w`** is the slope: how many extra minutes each kilometre adds.
- **`b`** is the intercept: the minutes it takes even if you live next door (cooking, boxing, finding the scooter keys).

Machine learning people call `w` and `b` **parameters**. Think of them as knobs. Our whole job is to find good settings for the knobs.

"Just eyeball it" works fine for 2 knobs. The models behind modern chatbots have *billions* of knobs. So we need a recipe that works for any number of knobs, without a human in the loop.

That recipe is **gradient descent**. Nearly every neural network you've heard of is trained with some souped-up version of it. Learn it once, and you understand the engine under the hood of modern AI.

### First, what does "good" even mean?

Before we can improve the knobs, we need a **score** for how wrong they are. That score is called the **loss**.

- Lower loss = better guesses. Like golf: you want the smallest score.
- A loss of 0 means the line passes exactly through every point. (With real, messy data, that never happens.)

We'll use the most common loss for predicting numbers, **mean squared error**:

1. For each delivery, compute the miss: *predicted − actual*.
2. **Square** it, so misses in both directions count as bad and big misses hurt *a lot* more than small ones (miss by 2 → 4, miss by 10 → 100).
3. Take the **average** over all deliveries.

```text
loss = average of (predicted − actual)²
```

That's it. That's the scary formula. Try it: change `w` and `b` below and see if you can push the loss down by hand.

<PyRunner code={lossCode} title="Score a guess" />

You probably got the loss down with a few tries. Now imagine doing that with a billion knobs. 😵 Let's teach the computer to do the fiddling.

## 2. 🌫️ The real-life analogy

<Analogy emoji="🌫️" title="Walking downhill in thick fog">

You're lost on a hillside in fog so thick you can't see your own shoes. You want to get down to the village in the valley.

You can't see the valley. But you *can* feel the ground under your feet. So you:

1. **Feel** which way the ground tilts down.
2. **Take a step** that way.
3. **Repeat** until the ground feels flat.

That's gradient descent. That's the whole algorithm.

</Analogy>

Here's how the analogy maps onto our pizza problem:

| In the fog | In machine learning |
| --- | --- |
| Where you're standing | The current values of the knobs (`w`, `b`) |
| Your altitude | The loss: how wrong you are |
| The tilt under your feet | The **slope**, a.k.a. the **gradient** |
| How long your stride is | The **learning rate** |
| The valley floor | The best knob settings: lowest loss |

### Feeling the slope with your feet

The slope answers one question: **"If I nudge this knob a tiny bit up, does the loss go up or down, and how fast?"**

You can literally compute it that way. Nudge `w` up a hair, nudge it down a hair, and see how much the loss changed:

<PyRunner code={slopeCode} title="Feel the slope by nudging" />

Two things to notice in the output:

- The nudging trick and the formula agree. Calculus is just a shortcut for nudging. (Mathematicians call the slope the **derivative**. With several knobs, the list of all their slopes is the **gradient**.)
- Both slopes are **negative**. Negative means "the loss goes *down* if you increase this knob". So downhill is *up* for both `w` and `b`.

### The update rule (the one formula you need)

The gradient points **uphill**. We want to go **downhill**, so we step the opposite way:

```text
new w = old w − learning_rate × slope
```

Read that minus sign carefully, because it does all the work:

- **Slope positive** (uphill is to the right): we subtract, so `w` moves **left**. ✅ Downhill.
- **Slope negative** (uphill is to the left): we subtract a negative, which *adds*, so `w` moves **right**. ✅ Downhill.
- **Steep slope**: big step. **Gentle slope**: small step. **Flat** (at the bottom): no step. The algorithm slows down by itself as it arrives. Neat.

<Analogy emoji="🦶" title="The learning rate is your stride">

- **Baby steps** (tiny learning rate): you'll get there… next Tuesday.
- **Sensible strides**: you get down quickly and stop at the bottom.
- **Giant leaps**: you overshoot the valley and land on the opposite hillside, maybe *higher* than where you started. Then you overshoot back. Then you're on the moon.

</Analogy>

<summary>🤓 Nerd corner: why chaos starts at exactly {facts.bowl.chaosLr} for this curve</summary>

- `lr` > {facts.bowl.chaosLr}: the factor is below −1. Every bounce is *bigger*. 💥

</details>

- **Local minimum**: lowest point *in the neighbourhood*.
- **Global minimum**: lowest point *anywhere*.

Ways out: start from somewhere else (**🎯 Good start**), or take bigger steps that hop over the hill (**🦘 Lucky leap**). Bigger steps are a gamble, though. Sometimes you hop into a better valley, sometimes off a cliff.

<Aside type="tip" title="Good news for later">
With real neural networks (millions of knobs), getting trapped in a *bad* local minimum turns out to be much less of a problem than this 1D picture suggests. Researchers are still working out exactly why. So learn the idea, but don't lose sleep over it yet.
</Aside>

<details>
<summary>🤓 Nerd corner: exactly how lopsided is this valley?</summary>

You can measure it. The **Hessian** is a small table of how fast the slope itself changes in each direction (the bowl's *curvature*). Its two **eigenvalues** are the curvature along the bowl's two main directions: straight across the valley, and straight along it.

<PyRunner code={valleyCode} title="Measure the valley" />

So the valley is about **{facts.valley.ratio}× steeper across than along**. A step of `lr × curvature` that's bigger than 2 overshoots by more than it started with, so the biggest learning rate that doesn't explode is **2 / {facts.valley.steep} ≈ {facts.valley.maxLr}**. That's why zig-zag ({facts.presets.zigzag}) survives and chaos ({facts.presets.chaos}) doesn't. (These numbers are recomputed and checked by the course's tests every time the lesson changes.)

</details>

### 🚀 Level up: smarter optimizers

Plain gradient descent (the "SGD" you'll meet in PyTorch) only looks at the slope *right now*. Two famous upgrades remember a little history:

- **Momentum** is a heavy ball: it keeps some of its previous speed, so it powers along gentle slopes (and sometimes rolls past the bottom).
- **Adam** rescales every step by how big the slopes have recently been, so its steps stay sensible on both steep walls and flat floors.

## 5. 🔥 The PyTorch way

Our slope formulas came from a bit of calculus on a straight line. For a neural network with millions of knobs, nobody writes those by hand. PyTorch does it for you. That feature is called **autograd** (automatic gradients).

Here's the cheat sheet. Four lines carry the whole lesson:

| PyTorch | What it means | Fog version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `requires_grad=True` | "Track how the loss depends on this knob." | Strap a tilt sensor to your boot. |
| `loss.backward()` | Compute the slope for every tracked knob, and store it in `.grad`. | Feel the ground. |
| `optimizer.step()` | Do `knob = knob − lr × knob.grad` for every knob. | Take the step. |
| `optimizer.zero_grad()` | Wipe the old slopes before computing new ones. | Forget the last spot's tilt. |

## 6. 🔧 "Bro, why is it broken?"

Every one of these has happened to every ML person ever. Including the people who wrote the textbooks.

### 💥 "My loss went to `inf` / `nan`"

**Cause:** learning rate too big. Every step overshoots by more than the last, until the numbers are too large for the computer to store.

<PyRunner code={tooBigCode} title="Watch it explode (NumPy)" />

PyTorch uses smaller (32-bit) numbers by default, so it blows up even sooner:

<Code code={bugNanTorch} lang="python" title="Same explosion in PyTorch" />

<Output text={bugNanTorchOut} />

**Fix:** divide the learning rate by 10 and try again. Once it stops exploding, nudge it back up. (Later you'll meet more fixes, such as normalizing your inputs.)

### 🐢 "My loss barely moves"

**Cause:** learning rate too small, or not enough steps. It's working, just at snail speed.

**Fix:** multiply the learning rate by 3 to 10 and watch the loss chart. You want a curve that drops fast, then flattens.

### 🏓 "My loss bounces up and down"

**Cause:** learning rate a bit too big. You're in "bouncy" territory, overshooting the bottom each step.

**Fix:** lower it a bit. A smooth, falling loss curve is the goal.

### 📈 "My loss goes *up*, steadily"

**Cause:** you wrote `w = w + lr * grad`. You built gradient **ascent**. Congratulations, you're now maximising wrongness.

<PyRunner code={wrongSignCode} title="One wrong sign" />

**Fix:** it's minus. Always minus.

### 🧺 "I forgot `optimizer.zero_grad()`"

PyTorch **adds** each new gradient to whatever's already in `.grad`, so it won't complain. It silently computes garbage:

<Code code={bugZeroGrad} lang="python" title="Gradients pile up" />

<Output text={bugZeroGradOut} />

It's like a shopping cart you never empty: every trip's items get added on top of the last. Your steps get bigger and more wrong as training goes on.

(Why does PyTorch add instead of replace? Because adding is genuinely useful for some advanced tricks. Just remember: **`zero_grad()` every step.**)

### 🚫 `RuntimeError: a leaf Variable that requires grad is being used in an in-place operation.`

You tried to update a tracked knob without `torch.no_grad()`:

<Code code={bugInplace} lang="python" title="The fix is one line" />

<Output text={bugInplaceOut} />

Using `optimizer.step()` avoids this entirely, since it handles `no_grad` for you.

### 🕳️ "It finished, but the answer is meh"

**Cause:** possibly stuck in a local minimum (or just not enough steps).

**Fix:** try a few different starting points and keep the best. You saw this in the bumpy sim.

<TLDR>

- **Loss** = one number for how wrong the model is. Lower is better.
- **Slope / gradient** = which way is uphill, and how steep. Found by "nudging", or by calculus, or by PyTorch's `loss.backward()`.
- **Update rule:** `w = w − learning_rate × slope`. The minus sign means "go downhill".
- **Learning rate** = stride length. Too small → slow. Too big → bouncy, then 💥 `nan`.
- **Local minima** exist. Different starting points can help.
- **PyTorch loop:** `zero_grad()` → compute loss → `backward()` → `step()`. Every. Single. Time.

</TLDR>
